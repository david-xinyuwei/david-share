## When training helps

Training adapts a pretrained model to your task with data for that task. It changes the model's weights; it does not add examples to each prompt. For a team with high-quality data for the task, the article names six uses:

- **Accuracy and relevance.** The model learns patterns from more examples than fit in a prompt.
- **Shorter prompts.** Fewer examples in the prompt lower token cost and latency.
- **Style and structure.** Answers keep one tone, format or schema.
- **Tool use.** Tool selection and the arguments of tool calls improve.
- **Retrieved context.** The model learns to use the relevant context and ignore the rest.
- **A smaller model.** Examples generated by a larger teacher model let a smaller model do the task at lower cost and latency.

None of these is guaranteed. Compare the trained model with your baseline on data you held out. Training adds training and hosting costs (see [Manage fine-tuning costs](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/fine-tuning/cost-management)). It replaces neither retrieval for current information nor the safety controls of your application.

## Training approaches

Foundry has two training approaches. Both run on training infrastructure that Foundry manages: you provision no training GPUs for either.

| Question | Managed Training | Programmed Training (preview) |
|---|---|---|
| Who runs the training loop | Foundry. You submit a job with your data and settings | Your Python program. Foundry executes the training and sampling operations that the program requests |
| Who it is for | Beginners, and AI engineers who prefer a predefined workflow to lower-level training operations | Experienced ML practitioners who build their own training workflow |
| What you control | The data, the hyperparameters the method supports, and the grader of an RFT job | Losses, rewards, rollouts, gradient accumulation, weight updates and checkpoints |
| Training methods | SFT, DPO and RFT, depending on the model; distillation by training on SFT data that a teacher model generated | Recipes for SFT, reinforcement learning, preference learning, distillation and custom losses |
| Examples the article gives | Distilling a larger model, learning from support conversations, improving answers with a grader | Collecting tool-use rollouts, applying your own rewards, changing how the loop updates and evaluates |

- Programmed Training is in preview and needs an approved access request before you can create a training session: [preview sign-up form](https://aka.ms/foundry-interactive-training-signup).
- The article's advice for someone new to model customization is to start with Managed Training.
- A model supported for one approach, method or modality is not automatically supported for another, and a recipe in the samples repository does not show that the service offers the combination. [Supported models](#supported-models) is the list that counts.

Terms: a rollout is one complete attempt of the model at a task; a grader is the component that scores a generated response.

More on the second approach: [What is Programmed Training (preview)?](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/fine-tuning/programmed-training/overview)

## Customization methods

A customization method defines what the model learns from. The three predefined methods belong to Managed Training. Programmed Training has no predefined methods: it exposes training primitives from which you build your own workflow.

| Method | What the model learns from | When it fits |
|---|---|---|
| **Supervised fine-tuning (SFT)** | Prompts, each with the response you want | You can show the behavior the model should learn |
| **Preference fine-tuning with direct preference optimization (DPO)** | A preferred and a rejected response to the same input | Quality is a matter of preference, such as tone or completeness, and there is no single right answer |
| **Reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT)** | Prompts and a grader that scores the responses the model generates | Several solutions are possible and you can score their quality reliably |

Guides on Microsoft Learn: [supervised fine-tuning](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/openai/how-to/fine-tuning), [direct preference optimization](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/openai/how-to/fine-tuning-direct-preference-optimization), [reinforcement fine-tuning](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/openai/how-to/reinforcement-fine-tuning).

## Training types

A training type says where a training job runs and which capacity, price and guarantees apply. It is separate from the deployment type, which says how the trained model is served.

| Training type | Where training runs | Price and guarantees |
|---|---|---|
| **Standard** | In the region of your Foundry resource | Data residency is guaranteed. For workloads whose data must stay in one region |
| **Data zone** | Inside a data zone, not in one region. Only the US data zone is supported | For workloads whose data must stay inside that zone |
| **Global** | On capacity outside your region. Your data and the weights are copied to the region where training runs | Lower price than Standard and no data residency. For workloads without a residency constraint that want shorter queue times |
| **Developer** | On idle capacity | Significant cost savings. No latency or SLA guarantee: a job can be preempted and resumed later. No data residency. For experiments and price-sensitive workloads |

## Deployment options

Foundry serves a trained model in one of two ways, with different serving infrastructure and billing:

- **Serverless.** Foundry hosts the fine-tuned model and runs the serving infrastructure.
- **Managed compute.** Dedicated GPUs run the matching base model with one or more compatible LoRA adapters. Serving adapters on Managed compute is a preview capability. This repository uses this option.

Which of the two a model can use depends on the model, the deployment option and what the training produced: see [Supported models](#supported-models). Serverless has five deployment types, which set where inference runs, the capacity and the billing:

| Serverless deployment type | Where inference runs | Billing and limits |
|---|---|---|
| **Standard** | In the region of the deployment | Per token, plus the hosting charge of a fine-tuned model where it applies |
| **Data Zone Standard** | Inside a data zone. Only the US data zone is supported | Per token |
| **Global Standard** | On global capacity. The custom weights may be stored outside the resource's geography for a time | Per token |
| **Developer** | Globally | Per token, with no hourly hosting fee. No data-residency guarantee and no availability SLA. For evaluation only: a deployment lasts 24 hours |
| **Provisioned Throughput** | On regional capacity | Provisioned throughput units (PTUs), for predictable throughput |

How to deploy: [Deploy fine-tuned models](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/fine-tuning/deploy-fine-tuned-models).

## Limits to plan for

The article describes training as an iterative engineering process, not a guaranteed improvement, and lists 8 things to plan for:

1. **Data quality and coverage.** Use examples that are accurate, consistent and representative. Before you upload data, look for missing scenarios, duplicates, bias and sensitive information.
2. **Overfitting and evaluation leakage.** Keep training, validation and final test data apart. Compare checkpoints on held-out tasks, not on training loss alone.
3. **Misleading reward signals.** Test an RFT grader with correct, incorrect and adversarial responses. A rising reward does not show better task performance when the model exploits the grader.
4. **Experimentation and cost.** Budget for repeated runs, grading, evaluation, hosting and inference. Stop experiments that do not improve the target metric.
5. **Model and serving compatibility.** Confirm the method, tier, version and deployment eligibility before you train. An adapter does not fit another base model with a similar name.
6. **Maintenance.** Reassess quality when the task data changes or the base model approaches retirement. Retraining and redeployment can become necessary.
7. **Safety and governance.** Review data handling and the managed safety evaluation. Keep the safety controls of your application and evaluate the runtime that finally serves the model.
8. **Programmed Training runtime responsibilities.** You maintain the driver, the environments, tokenization, checkpoints and recovery logic. Sampling inside a training session does not prove how the model behaves when it is served in production.
